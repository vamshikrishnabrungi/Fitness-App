# Macro Plan Reference Research Brief for SFTC

## Executive summary

Across the sports reviewed, high-quality macro planning follows the same backbone even when the sport changes: a general preparation phase builds tissue capacity, fundamental strength, movement quality, and aerobic or work-capacity foundations; a specific preparation phase shifts toward sport-speed, power, repeat-effort ability, and technical specificity; the competitive phase keeps intensity but trims volume, narrows exercise menus, and organizes harder work farther from match or competition day; and active-rest or return phases rebuild the athlete before the next climb. This logic appears in official football practice models, cross-country and track annual plans, tennis endurance guidance, cricket bowling management, weightlifting coaching education, and long-term athlete development statements. citeturn24view0turn22view2turn25view1turn20view2turn23view2

For SFTC, the most useful lesson is that “macro plan creation” should not start from workouts. It should start from constraints: season phase, sport demands, training age, current injury or pain status, competition calendar, and available recovery. Elite environments repeatedly show that coaches balance sport work and gym work by protecting key qualities rather than trying to train everything hard at once. When schedules compress, they reduce volume before they reduce intensity; when athletes are young or underprepared, they bias the plan toward exposure tolerance, movement quality, and staged progression; and when injury risk is high, they modify load distribution rather than abandoning development entirely. citeturn27view0turn27view1turn22view3turn20view2turn23view2

The backend implication is clear: SFTC should treat macro plans as rule-driven systems with different templates for foundation, strength base, power conversion, speed and agility, in-season maintenance, and return-to-training. Those templates should be sport-filtered, but the engine underneath them should be phase-aware, readiness-aware, and competition-aware. citeturn24view1turn25view2turn26view0turn23view2

## Cross-sport planning logic

A strong cross-sport pattern is phased sequencing. In the NSCA annual framework for collegiate distance runners, the year explicitly moves through active rest, general preparation, special preparation, and multiple competitive periods, with physical-quality priorities changing by phase rather than staying constant. ALTIS describes a similar early-season-to-competition progression in sprint training, while FIFA’s fitness-coach model emphasizes that the physical plan must cover the whole season, not merely the next match. Weightlifting guidance makes the same point in a different language: exercises, loads, and volumes should be sequenced across blocks, and technique development must remain central as loading changes. citeturn24view0turn23view4turn27view0turn26view0

A second pattern is microcycle organization around competition. FIFA’s Mexico U15 matchday-minus-one session is activation-heavy and tactical: mobility, core, speed, coordination, patterns of play, and set pieces, rather than exhaustive loading. Mamelodi Sundowns’ matchday-plus-three session shows the opposite end of the week: it acts as a bridge day that includes injury-prevention work, physical development, and tactical content while still respecting recovery from the previous match. In endurance sport, formal taper guidance is even more explicit: maintain intensity, preserve most training frequency, and reduce volume substantially so fatigue drops without detraining. Combat-sport tapering literature supports the same high-intensity/lower-volume logic. citeturn25view0turn22view2turn25view1turn25view2

A third pattern is interference management. The distance-running framework explicitly recommends block periodization and microdosed programming because endurance athletes already carry high aerobic loads and can suffer interference if too many competing qualities are pushed at once. Soccer literature similarly shows that strength/power work must be integrated with soccer-specific power exposures and that session frequency is adjusted across pre-season and in-season. In cricket, bowling frequency becomes the dominant stressor, so “extra” work has to fit around bowling day counts, spell lengths, and recovery windows. In other words, the backend should know which quality is already being taxed by the sport itself before prescribing more gym stress in the same direction. citeturn24view1turn22view1turn20view2

A fourth pattern is monitoring and individual adjustment. FIFA’s training-load guidance distinguishes internal and external load and warns against overreacting to isolated data points; Athletic Club’s academy monitors growth and maturity to adjust youth loads and uses bio-banded gym work when needed; Cricket Australia’s junior bowling guidance adjusts frequency, recovery, and even following-week loads after high exposure; and the UFC Performance Institute organizes strength and conditioning, nutrition, sports medicine, psychology, and sport science as an integrated support system rather than a single-program model. SFTC should therefore treat fatigue, growth, pain, schedule congestion, and travel as plan modifiers, not as afterthought notes. citeturn27view1turn22view3turn20view2turn27view3

## Sport reference cases

**Running and endurance sports — collegiate cross-country and track distance model plus elite middle-distance practice.** The most stable macro pattern is long-horizon sequencing. The NSCA framework for Division II women’s cross-country and track-distance athletes uses active rest, GPP, SPP, and competition blocks across the academic year, and rotates objectives among hypertrophy, endurance, strength, power, mobility, and speed instead of chasing only mileage. The same framework recommends wave loading and step progression for first-year or transfer athletes, microdosed in-season lifting for recovery, and block periodization to reduce interference between high aerobic work and strength-power work. World Athletics’ middle-distance nutrition framework notes that elite middle-distance athletes may complete two to three sessions per day, including track work, easy running, and weights, which reinforces how much recovery capacity determines program design at advanced levels. Close to races, taper guidance is consistent: keep intensity, reduce volume, preserve most frequency. The most useful tests in this model are movement and mobility screens, jump tests, and aerobic-power testing rather than strength numbers alone. For SFTC, the extracted rule is: beginners start with step progression, movement quality, modest strength, and aerobic consistency; intermediates can use block-oriented strength and power development; advanced athletes need microdosed strength and careful interference management rather than more generic volume. citeturn24view0turn24view1turn14search2turn25view1

**Football and soccer — FIFA matchday systems plus soccer strength-periodization literature.** Soccer planning is defined by the match calendar. FIFA’s matchday-minus-one example emphasizes activation, core work, mobility, speed, coordination, tactical patterns, and set pieces, which is a strong real-world reference for low-fatigue sharpening. FIFA’s matchday-plus-three case from Mamelodi Sundowns shows how a “bridge” day can combine injury-prevention circuits, physical development, and tactical rehearsal when the weekly schedule allows it. The role of the fitness coach, in FIFA’s own description, is to build a season-long plan that remains aligned to the head coach’s playing model while staying flexible around injuries, fixture congestion, and last-minute decisions. In the peer-reviewed soccer literature, two weekly strength sessions are typically enough to improve force and power in pre-season, while one weekly session is generally enough to avoid in-season detraining; the same review argues that strength/power work should increasingly blend with soccer-specific power actions as competition approaches. Youth planning must be adjusted for growth and maturity, because Athletic Club’s academy work shows that somatic maturity changes injury risk patterns and justifies load modification and bio-banded gym exposure. The backend rule here is: pre-season can support two meaningful gym doses plus broader speed/power development; in-season usually shifts to one maintenance lift, one optional top-up lift for non-starters, and matchday-minus-one should avoid high-volume lower-body work. citeturn25view0turn22view2turn27view0turn22view1turn22view3

**Cricket — Pat Cummins’ rebuild plus Cricket Australia junior pace-bowling rules.** Cricket planning, especially for fast bowlers, is dominated by exposure management. Cricket Australia’s junior pace-bowling guidance gives unusually usable backend logic: under-17 bowlers should aim for at least one day off bowling after a bowling day, a maximum of three bowling days per week, and five-over spells; players aged 17–19 should avoid more than two bowling days in a row, cap at four bowling days per week, and use six-over spells. The same guidance recommends avoiding consecutive days where possible, scheduling one light bowling week every four to six weeks, taking a full week off bowling every 10–12 weeks, and allowing a more extended off-bowling period at season’s end before gradually rebuilding in pre-season. It also flags vulnerable bowlers—those with prior lumbar bone stress injury, recent growth spurts, or unusually high speed and size relative to peers—for more conservative modification. Pat Cummins’ rebuild offers the advanced counterpart: after repeated injuries, he describes a six-month pre-season with very specific goals and low-intensity bowling three times per week for five to six overs at the top of off stump for months, prioritizing durable accumulation over urgency. A survey of Australian coaches reinforces the planning emphasis by showing that fast bowlers have the highest injury rates in cricket and that physical conditioning and workload monitoring are the primary prevention levers. For SFTC, the rule is: bowling load is the first-class citizen; gym work supports trunk, hip, gluteal, and posterior-chain resilience and should never compromise managed bowling exposures. citeturn20view2turn20view1turn21search0

**Volleyball — elite in-season maintenance plus LTAD stage control.** Volleyball is a repeated-jump, repeated-overhead, change-of-direction sport, so macro plans are shaped by tendon and shoulder management more than by generic conditioning. The classic elite female in-season case study reports that ten senior professional players completed two weekly sessions combining resistance training and plyometrics during a 12-week season, a useful reference for maintenance or even improvement during competition rather than simple preservation. A season-long study in elite volleyball players also tracked perceived load, soreness, stress, fatigue, sleep quality, and the Hooper Index across preparatory, regular, and congested weeks, underlining that week type—not just session design—should change the plan. Volleyball Canada’s Development Matrix adds practical high-performance boundaries: at advanced stages, total exposure can reach as high as 11 sessions per week, such as six practices, three physical sessions, and two matches, but athletes should still have a full day off each week and at least five hours between same-day practices. Volleyball Canada’s skills matrix also organizes learning by LTAD stage, which is a direct reminder that beginners should spend more macro time on technical and tactical learning than on dense physical loading. For SFTC, the extracted rule is: in-season gym work is realistic at roughly two sessions per week for established players, but jump count, shoulder load, and match density must gate lower-body plyometrics and heavy eccentric work. citeturn28search1turn18search13turn28search17turn29search8turn18search17

**Basketball — NCAA off-season body-transformation block, in-season maintenance, and youth rest rules.** Furman men’s basketball provides a clear off-season macro example: eight weeks of summer training with both on-court practice and resistance training, framed as the second-longest uninterrupted block of the year, with body transformation and physical prep for a fast style of play as the central objective. The same case explicitly individualizes by training age, position, body type, and playing-time demands. In season, research in basketball settings commonly reports practical structures around five practices, two resistance sessions, and two games in a week. For youth, the NBA/USA Basketball guidance is even more backend-ready: recommended practice frequency rises from one practice per week at ages 7–8 to three to four practices per week in grades 9–12; minimum rest days fall from two per week in younger groups to one per week in adolescents; and tournament schedules with multiple games per day should be followed by extra recovery time. Human Kinetics’ in-season recommendation of one to two total-body resistance sessions per week matches the broader maintenance logic seen in basketball environments. For SFTC, that yields a clean rule set: youth plans must enforce rest days and exposure caps; college and pro plans should individualize by minutes and role; and in-season strength should usually be brief, total-body, and maintenance-oriented rather than a separate fatigue-heavy training stream. citeturn22view0turn17search0turn22view5turn17search7

**Combat sports — integrated support, fight-camp tapering, and interference control.** Combat sport macro plans are unusually sensitive to the interaction between technical training, sparring, maximal-intent conditioning, body-mass management, and psychological stress. The UFC Performance Institute’s model is revealing because it is explicitly interdisciplinary: strength and conditioning, sports medicine, nutrition, sport science, psychology, and athlete lifestyle support are all treated as linked performance services, not independent add-ons. In the research literature, professional boxing reviews and combat-conditioning reviews both stress that physical preparation has to be specific to the sport’s energy-system profile and injury risks, while MMA research shows that short-term, high-intensity, low-volume sport-specific strength and conditioning can improve physical fitness without relying on bodybuilding-style gym structure. Tapering evidence for competition recommends approximately eight to 14 days with a progressive training-load reduction of roughly 41–60% while maintaining intensity and much of the frequency. For SFTC, the planning logic is straightforward: do not stack hard sparring, maximal lower-body lifting, and aggressive weight-cutting in the same short window; use fight-camp blocks to shift from general strength toward power, alactic or repeated high-intensity work, and technical specificity; and make return-to-training plans especially conservative after injury or severe weight stress. citeturn27view3turn11search22turn11search23turn11search15turn25view2

**Tennis — endurance block logic plus touring-team development.** ITF’s endurance guidance gives very practical planning references. Extensive continuous endurance sessions are recommended at 30–60 minutes, three to four times per week within an endurance block, but only at least once per week during tournament season; intensive continuous work typically lasts 20–30 minutes and should not be done more than twice weekly because it compromises recovery and increases overtraining risk. Heart rate is recommended as a simple training-control tool. At the developmental level, the ITF Touring Team model shows how good systems organize training camps and tournaments together rather than separately: players from developing nations travel in team environments, receive access to high-performance coaches, and have a dedicated strength and conditioning coach while alternating training camps and tournament blocks on different surfaces. The ITF’s recognition of the LTA National Tennis Centre further highlights the integrated model of courts, gym, medical support, and coach collaboration from U12 through high performance. For SFTC, that means tennis macro plans should be surface- and tournament-aware, should not overdose conditioning during dense tournament windows, and should progress from aerobic base and movement skill toward more surface-specific, competition-specific loading as the athlete advances. citeturn22view4turn27view2turn8search1

**Olympic weightlifting — technique-first sequencing across blocks.** Weightlifting is unusual because the “gym” is the sport, so sport-practice and strength-work separation largely disappears. USA Weightlifting’s coaching pathway explicitly teaches training principles and periodization at Level 2, and USAW’s coaching guidance stresses the importance of recovery periods and incremental increases in training intensity and load. The IWF’s beginner coaching courses similarly identify development of strength, periodization, beginner training programs, and year planning as core teaching content. The NSCA position statement on weightlifting for sports performance adds the high-level planning logic: weightlifting movements and derivatives can improve force, RFD, and power more effectively than general resistance training or plyometrics alone in many athletic contexts, but they need to be programmed sequentially across blocks with exercise variation, loads, and volumes chosen for the desired adaptation, while technique and skill development remain central. The backend implication is clean: beginner-development lifters should be placed in technique-first plans with careful load increments and limited novelty; intermediate lifters can accumulate more frequent technical exposures; and advanced lifters can use more concentrated blocks that emphasize strength-speed or speed-strength without abandoning technical quality. citeturn12search3turn12search6turn12search11turn26view0turn26view1

**Track and field sprinting and jumping — double peaks, speed preservation, and parallel strength themes.** World Athletics’ annual sprint model describes a typical double-periodized year aimed at separate peaks, while World Athletics’ sprint materials and round-table discussion emphasize that sprint performance depends on maximal speed quality, speed endurance, and highly specific technical organization. ALTIS’ strength-programming model is useful because it shows how an advanced sprint environment can target multiple strength themes at once via complex-parallel periodization: in one example with athletes who already have extensive training age, strength work occurred four days per week with two explosive-strength sessions, one hypertrophy/work-capacity session, and one maximal-strength session. Crucially, the heavier or more metabolically costly zones were followed by active or passive recovery, and explosive work was used as a potentiator rather than a fatigue driver. For SFTC, the takeaway is that sprint and jump macro plans should preserve max-velocity freshness, place explosive work before more fatiguing work in the week, and reserve high-volume strength building for periods farther from the key competition. Beginners should stay closer to World Athletics’ broad run-jump-throw literacy model; advanced sprinters and jumpers can handle much more parallel quality development. citeturn13search3turn13search14turn23view4turn15search6

**General strength and athletic development — the LTAD backbone.** The NSCA LTAD position statement is the clearest general-development reference in the set: it argues for a structured, logical, evidence-based, individualized, and child-centered approach because modern youth are often either underprepared for sport or overloaded by competition without preparatory conditioning. The IOC youth athletic-development consensus makes the same broad case for healthy, resilient, and capable youth athletes, while World Athletics Kids’ Athletics emphasizes broad exposure to sprinting, endurance running, jumping, and throwing at early stages. Put simply, beginners should not be trained like diluted elite athletes. They need movement vocabulary, physical literacy, simple strength progressions, and enough variety to build durable adaptability. Intermediates can start emphasizing force production and specific capacities. Advanced athletes can specialize harder because they already own the base. For SFTC, this is the master template behind every sport-specific branch. citeturn23view2turn15search8turn15search6

## Progression and adjustment model

The most defensible beginner-to-intermediate progression is not “add more days” but “increase exposure complexity only after exposure tolerance exists.” The distance-running framework recommends wave loading and step progression for newer athletes; volleyball and basketball youth systems cap practice density and preserve rest days; cricket imposes bowling-day and spell-length ceilings by age; and LTAD guidance explicitly warns against asking underprepared athletes to tolerate competition-heavy calendars without preparatory conditioning. For SFTC, a user with low training history, poor movement quality, recent pain, or low readiness should therefore begin with a foundation block that prioritizes movement competency, submaximal strength, aerobic base, and tissue-capacity work before power conversion or sport-speed emphasis. citeturn24view1turn22view5turn20view2turn23view2

The intermediate-to-advanced progression is best understood as a shift from general loading to selective loading. In soccer, this means keeping one to two high-value strength doses and layering more soccer-specific power work. In volleyball and basketball, it means maintaining force and power while match exposures already provide much of the sport stress. In sprinting and weightlifting, it means using more concentrated blocks or more parallel development because the athlete already has the technical competence and training age to tolerate them. In tennis and combat sports, it means the athlete’s calendar drives the plan much more strongly, so strength and conditioning becomes microdosed and support-oriented rather than a separate “main event.” citeturn22view1turn28search1turn17search7turn23view4turn26view0turn22view4turn27view3

Adjustment rules for fatigue, travel, and injury are also consistent across sports. FIFA recommends long-term trend analysis and human judgment rather than single-metric micromanagement; Athletic Club modifies youth loads around growth and maturity; the NBA youth model adds recovery after dense tournament play; cricket explicitly reduces following-week bowling after high workloads and prescribes deload weeks; and combat-sport high-performance support treats sleep, nutrition, and lifestyle stress as part of performance management rather than separate wellness topics. For SFTC, that means backend logic should respond to schedule compression, disrupted sleep, pain flags, travel, recent layoff, and spikes in sport volume by reducing lower-body volume first, limiting novelty second, and preserving a small amount of quality exposure whenever safe. citeturn27view1turn22view3turn22view5turn20view2turn27view3

## SFTC backend rules extracted

**rule_beginner_foundation_default.** If the user is new, underprepared, recently detrained, or reporting pain/low readiness, default to a foundation block emphasizing movement quality, basic strength patterns, mobility, aerobic base, and conservative step progression. Delay high-volume plyometrics, max-speed exposures, and complex Olympic variations until tissue tolerance and movement quality improve. citeturn23view2turn24view1turn15search6

**rule_strength_base_before_power_conversion.** If the sport requires jumping, sprinting, throwing, striking, or rapid COD but the athlete lacks a demonstrable strength base, prioritize force production and tissue capacity first; convert toward power only after a base exists. This is consistent with soccer strength literature, sprint/ALTIS sequencing, and weightlifting guidance on sequential programming. citeturn22view1turn23view4turn26view0

**rule_matchday_minus_1_low_fatigue.** If the athlete has a match or competition within 24 hours, avoid high-volume lower-body strength, exhaustive conditioning, heavy eccentric overload, and novelty. Recommended emphasis: mobility, activation, coordination, speed primer, tactical rehearsal, and set pieces or skill sharpness. citeturn25view0turn25view1

**rule_in_season_maintain_intensity_reduce_volume.** During in-season maintenance, keep intensity exposure for strength and power but reduce total volume and exercise menu size. Use one to two short total-body gym sessions per week in court and field sports when the schedule allows; use even smaller microdoses when match congestion is high. citeturn22view1turn28search1turn17search7turn22view2

**rule_concurrent_interference_guardrail.** Do not place exhaustive endurance work and maximal power/strength work as equally hard competing stressors in the same short window unless the plan is intentionally concurrent and recovery has been protected. Endurance-athlete block models and soccer concurrent-training evidence both argue for gating this combination carefully. citeturn24view1turn16search6

**rule_bowling_load_priority.** For cricket fast bowlers, bowling exposure determines the week. Enforce age-appropriate bowling-day counts, spell limits, and non-bowling days; reduce gym stress if bowling stress is rising; add light weeks every four to six weeks and a full week off after 10–12 weeks of sustained bowling. citeturn20view2

**rule_youth_growth_and_maturity_modifier.** For adolescent users in rapid growth or with maturity-related risk flags, reduce impact and high-speed loading, bias toward technique and controlled strength work, and consider bio-banded or maturity-aware progression. This is particularly important in football academies and other sports where PHV changes injury risk. citeturn22view3turn23view2

**rule_taper_template_endurance_and_individual_sport.** For important competitions in endurance and individual sports, preserve intensity, keep at least roughly 80% of normal frequency, and reduce volume substantially. Use shorter tapers for shorter events and longer tapers for longer events; a progressive taper pattern is usually preferable. citeturn25view1turn25view2

**rule_tournament_density_recovery.** If the schedule contains multiple games in a day or on consecutive days, the plan should insert additional recovery time after the event and should avoid adding conditioning simply because gym sessions become shorter. Youth basketball guidance and congested-week volleyball monitoring both support schedule-aware recovery protection. citeturn22view5turn18search13

**rule_return_to_training_conservative_rebuild.** After injury, pain flare, or long layoff, reintroduce exposure through lower-volume, lower-complexity work and gradual, repeated submaximal practice rather than immediate return to peak intensity. Cummins’ bowling rebuild and LTAD foundations both support this conservative rebuild logic. citeturn20view1turn23view2

**rule_monitor_trends_not_single_points.** Readiness and load algorithms should weight trends over isolated data points. Session RPE, heart rate, jump markers, wellness scores, bowling exposures, and maturity data are useful, but they are decision supports rather than automatic prescriptions. citeturn27view1turn18search13turn20view2

**rule_stage_specific_competition_ratio.** In developmental stages, bias macro time toward training over competition; at advanced stages, competition takes a larger share of the calendar and training becomes more targeted. Volleyball Canada’s LTAD model and broader LTAD literature make this progression explicit. citeturn29search0turn29search3turn23view2
